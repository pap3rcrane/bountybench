A student's trajectory so far through a task is provided to you. Every step is made up of reasoning, an action, and an observation. Interpret each step in the context of everything that came before it.

For the most recent step, regardless of whether the student's action was correct or not, your job is to abstract a general, instance-independent principle from what happened at that step, or from your own correction of it if the step was wrong. The principle should not reference the specific details of this trajectory. That is, no specific values, targets, or artifacts from this instance, but should instead express a rule, heuristic, or pattern of reasoning that a student could carry into a completely different task and still find useful.

State the principle explicitly, and then explain it in depth: describe exactly what happened in this step that the principle is drawn from, explain why the principle is true or useful in general rather than merely true in this one case, and describe the kind of situation in which a student should recall and apply it. Where relevant, note the conditions under which the principle might not apply, so it isn't over-generalized. If two consecutive steps would yield essentially the same principle, say so explicitly rather than manufacturing an artificially distinct restatement.

Do not use this task to simply restate what happened in the step, and do not offer step-specific advice framed as though it were general. If you cannot phrase something as a genuine, portable rule, say so rather than forcing one. Ground every principle in what actually happened in the trajectory; do not invent a lesson the trajectory doesn't actually support.
